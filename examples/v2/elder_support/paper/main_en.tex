\begin{center}\textbf{Abstract}\end{center}
\small
Loneliness and social isolation among older adults living alone are shared concerns of social work, social security and social governance. Communities support these elders through three main channels: professional social-worker visits, mutual-aid pairing of younger-old with older-old residents (organised through a time bank), and volunteer matching through a digital platform. Two practice questions remain hard to answer: do ties survive once a service is withdrawn, and when is entry least costly? In the real world the same elders cannot both receive and not receive an intervention. We build a layered agent-based simulation on AgentSociety2 as a policy sandbox: six LLM-driven focal elders are embedded in a 20-person rules-layer support network, profiles are generated from CLHLS 2021 and CHARLS survey distributions, and the three interventions run in months 7--18 and are then withdrawn for six months. Methodologically, we contribute a transferable scientific discipline: preregistration and validity gates, root-cause classification of failures, cost accounting including sunk costs, a ledger stating whether each result is fixed by rules or parameters, and dynamics calibration accepted on held-out targets. Three main results follow. (1) \textbf{Data anchoring works}: survey-based profiles beat ordinary random ones on input fidelity; after calibrating loneliness dynamics, the held-out three-year persistence error falls from 43.1 to 4.6 percentage points, and survey-anchored profiles beat random ones on output error (4.3 vs.\ 7.3 pp). (2) \textbf{Scenario projection}: social-worker visits are strongest in-window (isolation \IsoIvCasework\% vs.\ a \IsoIvNone\% baseline) and fall back to the never-treated level after withdrawal; the post-withdrawal advantage of mutual-aid pairing depends entirely on the pairing-persistence parameter and vanishes when it is zeroed; the ranking is the same across profile sources. (3) \textbf{A cross-model comparison exposed an environment defect}: a more boundary-respecting model went largely silent on the old router, which turned out to phrase ``cannot write in this call'' as ``cannot contact anyone''; after the fix it acts. Case results are projections under explicit parameters for generating testable practice hypotheses, not effect estimates for real populations; every number reproduces from released scripts.\par\vspace{0.4em}

\section{Introduction}
Loneliness and social isolation among Chinese adults aged 65+ living alone are large in scale: in the CLHLS 2008/09 wave, 52\% of those living alone reported feeling lonely, nearly twice the rate among those not living alone (29.5\%)\citep{wei2022living} (background only; our calibration uses the 2021 wave and the ``often/always'' definition, so the figures are not directly comparable). Communities respond through three main channels belonging to three policy fields: \textbf{social-worker visits} (social work: professional outreach by risk tier), \textbf{mutual-aid pairing} (social security: mutual-aid elder care in which healthy younger-old residents are paired with older-old ones and sustained by time-bank credits), and \textbf{volunteer matching} (social governance: volunteers dispatched on demand through a digital platform in community governance). The three are often combined in practice, yet three questions remain hard to answer empirically:

\textbf{(1) Do ties survive once a service is withdrawn?} Evaluations almost always measure outcomes during the programme or at its end\citep{lu2024timebank}; the counterfactual trajectory after withdrawal cannot be observed, because the same elders cannot both receive and not receive an intervention.

\textbf{(2) When is entry least costly?} Waiting lists and budgets make start timing a real policy variable, yet its marginal cost has never been systematically quantified.

\textbf{(3) Can such a simulation be trusted?} Generative agents\citep{park2023generative} produce reasoned decisions in a controlled environment and allow ``parallel worlds'' to be replayed under the same seed, which suits the first two questions; but they face a threefold challenge: are the data fabricated, are the conclusions reproducible, and are the ``findings'' merely echoes of the environment's rules?

Using social support for older adults living alone as the case, we build a method around the third question and then use it to project the first two. Our contributions are threefold:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{A transferable scientific discipline}: preregistered hypotheses and validity gates, archiving and root-cause classification of failed runs, cost accounting including sunk costs, and a \textbf{result-source ledger} stating which results follow directly from rules and parameters (Table~\ref{tab:prescribed}).
  \item \textbf{Data anchoring with held-out validation}: profiles are generated from survey distributions, and a preregistered ablation shows they are closer to reality than ordinary random ones; loneliness dynamics are calibrated by the propose--evaluate loop on training targets and accepted on held-out targets (Section~\ref{sec:calib}).
  \item \textbf{A layered policy sandbox}: an LLM focal layer embedded in a zero-API-cost rules layer, with intervention mechanism, intensity, timing and post-withdrawal persistence as externalised parameters supporting large scenario scans and zeroing tests; a layer decomposition quantifies each layer's contribution (Table~\ref{tab:layers}).
\end{enumerate}
Case results are scenario projections under explicit parameters, used to demonstrate the method and to generate testable practice hypotheses. Core conclusions and innovations are summarised in Section~\ref{sec:core}; reviewers' concerns and the corresponding sections are mapped in Appendix Table~\ref{tab:review}.

\section{Related Work and Theoretical Basis}
\textbf{Social support theory and the convoy model.} The convoy model\citep{kahn1980convoys} views an older person's social ties as layered convoys moving through the life course: the inner circle (spouse, children) is stable but eroded by widowhood and health shocks, while the outer circle (neighbours, friends) is more fluid. Our relationship ledger operationalises this structure directly: every tie has a strength, a type, and a most-recent-contact month, and decays when contact lapses.

\textbf{The empirical shape of tie decay.} Burt's cross-study fits show that decay rates are non-constant and follow a power function---``older ties are more stable''\citep{burt2000decay}; tie termination among older adults has also been measured directly\citep{kleinikkink1999broken}. In Section~\ref{sec:honest} we measure our model's survival curves against Burt's yardstick and report the structural deviation candidly.

\textbf{Effect-size evidence for loneliness interventions.} The meta-analysis by Masi et al.\ provides an effect-size yardstick for four intervention classes (social-cognitive interventions largest, $d=-0.598$)\citep{masi2011meta}; the subtype breakdown of Hoang et al.\ shows technology-based interventions with CIs crossing zero and multi-component interventions significant in community settings\citep{hoang2022interventions}. Two casework RCTs (older adults living alone\citep{ristolainen2020effects}; community-dwelling frail elders\citep{taube2018case}) point in the same direction with conservative ITT estimates. Quasi-experimental timebank evidence indicates effects lasting at least to programme end with no crowding-out\citep{lu2024timebank}. This literature supplies the direction and relative strength of the three interventions; our intervention parameter values have not yet been aligned with these effect sizes (Section~\ref{sec:interventions}).

\textbf{Generative-agent simulation.} Park et al.\ established the paradigm of LLM agents producing believable social behaviour\citep{park2023generative}. This paper differs in its policy-comparison purpose: a preregistered experimental design, calibration to real survey data, and systematic validity checking.

\section{Method: Layered Hybrid Simulation}
\subsection{Overall architecture}
The simulation is built on the AgentSociety2 framework (Ray parallelism, full tracing, replayable decisions). Twenty older adults living alone form a support network; six of them are \textbf{LLM focal elders} (PersonAgent, model gpt-5.6-sol) who, each month, run a multi-round tool loop to perceive the environment, retrieve memory, act, and articulate a natural-language rationale. The remaining fourteen residents and all network dynamics (tie decay, monthly events, intervention mechanics) are driven by the \textbf{rules layer} and consume no LLM calls. The focal layer supplies mechanistic and narrative evidence; the rules layer preserves the integrity of the network structure and supports zero-cost large-sample sensitivity scans. Measured call density is roughly 13.8 calls per focal elder per month. \textbf{The two layers generate contact differently}: rules-layer elders automatically receive contact each month according to their child-contact frequency and extroversion; focal elders receive contact only through their own tool actions (calling family, visiting neighbours, seeking help, attending activities) and through visits by intervention actors---a month in which the LLM takes no action is recorded as a month without contact. This difference is the direct cause of the threefold gap in isolation levels between layers reported in Section~\ref{sec:layers}, and it means the focal-layer isolation rate measures both ``is the elder isolated'' and ``did the LLM act''. Figure~\ref{fig:arch} shows the overall structure.

\subsection{Environment router}\label{sec:router}
Focal elders cannot call environment tools directly. They can only send a one-sentence natural-language request through \texttt{ask\_env}, declaring whether it is read-only (observing) or write (acting). The code-generation router passes the sentence and the tool signatures available in that mode to another model, which writes call code that is executed in a sandbox; the result returns to the elder in natural language. Registered tools come in two kinds: read-only tools (observing one's own situation, the community report) and write tools (calling, visiting, asking for help, joining activities); read-only requests can only use the former. This layer is the only interface between model and environment, so how it states capability boundaries directly affects whether agents act. Before 2026-10-20, read-only answers did not distinguish ``cannot write in this call'' from ``no such function''; after the fix, questions about whether a function exists are answered from the registry (``the tool exists; this read-only call cannot invoke it; use readonly=false to act''), and any generated answer still denying a registered tool is replaced and not cached (the incident is described in Section~\ref{sec:robust}).

\subsection{Environment and the relationship ledger}
The environment module (ElderSupportEnv) maintains a relationship ledger for every elder: tie type (child / neighbour / friend / volunteer / social worker), strength $s\in[0,1]$, monthly contact frequency, and most recent contact month. A tie with no contact in a given month decays as $s \leftarrow s\times(1-\delta)$ (baseline $\delta=0.06$; sensitivity scan 0.15/0.25) and is deleted at $s<0.05$. Monthly events are of three kinds: illness and falls (both rising with age and lowering health) and a neighbour or friend moving away (deleting that tie), with baseline monthly rates of 0.10, 0.03 and 0.02; these rates are modelling assumptions (appendix parameter table). All simulated elders live alone and no spouse is modelled, so there is no widowhood event. Contact with children is not an event; it is generated each month from the contact frequency of each child tie. Loneliness $\ell\in[0,1]$ relaxes each month by half towards a steady state set by the month's emotional contacts, tie count, health and extroversion (appendix parameter table); the formula has no event term, and events enter only indirectly through health. Loneliness is therefore a smoothed function of the isolation rate, not an independent outcome. The cut-off $\ell\ge0.6$ is hand-set and not calibrated against the survey distribution, so it is not treated as an aligned target.

\subsection{The three interventions}\label{sec:interventions}
\textbf{Selection logic.} The three are not social work's three practice methods (casework, group work, community work), nor do they sit at the same level: social-worker visits are a professional service, mutual-aid pairing is a reciprocity mechanism carried by a time bank, and volunteer matching is volunteer service brokered by a digital platform. In what follows we refer to the three conditions by their code names casework, timebank and platform. What we compare is one dimension they share: which layer of an elder's support network they supplement, and what relational logic sustains it. Within the convoy model\citep{kahn1980convoys}, social-worker outreach injects a formal support node and tries to repair inner-circle family ties, corresponding to formal support filling in where informal support is missing in the hierarchical compensatory model\citep{cantor1979neighbors}; the timebank builds reciprocal peer ties in the middle circle, sustained by the norm of reciprocity\citep{gouldner1960norm}; the platform matches volunteers on demand in the outer circle, supplying weak ties\citep{granovetter1973weak}. The three logics correspond to ``who fills in'', ``can it sustain itself'' and ``can it reach on demand'', which is why they suit a comparison of whether ties persist after withdrawal. In policy terms the three belong to social work (professional outreach), social security (mutual-aid elder care organised through credits) and social governance (volunteer platforms in community governance), covering the three main channels through which communities support older adults living alone. In practice the three are often combined; we compare them separately to identify mechanisms, not because they are mutually exclusive or interchangeable. Table~\ref{tab:mechanism} lists, for each intervention, the need addressed, the service actions as implemented in code, the relational mechanism, the post-withdrawal condition and the degree of implementation.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{casework --- social-worker visits (risk-targeted outreach)}: after risk ranking, social workers visit high-risk elders and reconnect them with family; top-down and precise, but constrained by caseload. Only three components of case management are implemented---risk screening, regular visits and family linking---with no needs assessment, service plan, follow-up or case closure, so we do not call it case management. The direction of effect follows the Masi meta-analysis\citep{masi2011meta} and the two casework RCTs\citep{ristolainen2020effects,taube2018case}; the coverage cap equals the number of workers $\times$ caseload.
  \item \textbf{timebank --- mutual-aid pairing (time bank)}: younger-old healthy elders are paired with high-risk elders and exchange services for credits under automatic rules; the pairings are real interpersonal ties and persist after withdrawal with probability post\_persist (midpoint 0.5; rules-layer scan 0--1 including a zero control, Table~\ref{tab:pp}). The mechanism follows the Hong Kong timebank quasi-experiment\citep{lu2024timebank}; no literature point estimate exists for post-withdrawal persistence, so it is treated as a scan.
  \item \textbf{platform --- volunteer matching (digital platform)}: responsive, broad-coverage, rotating volunteers, shallow ties. Its effect is set weakest of the three, with the direction following meta-analytic evidence that technology-based interventions have CIs crossing zero\citep{hoang2022interventions}.
\end{itemize}
\textbf{Nature of the parameters.} The literature supplies only the direction and relative strength of the three interventions; their specific probabilities (e.g.\ 0.8 that a social-worker visit counts as emotional contact, 0.5 for a family link, 0.85 that a timebank visit takes place in a month, a 0.35 cap on platform-volunteer tie strength) are modelling choices, not converted from published effect sizes; values are listed in the appendix parameter table. The ratio of the three interventions' effect sizes should therefore not be read as an estimate of real-world service effects.

\textbf{Unequal inputs.} The three interventions' service volumes are not equalised: social-worker outreach covers a fixed workers $\times$ caseload $=16$ elders each month (80\% of the 20); the timebank is limited by the number of available helpers; the platform dispatches only to elders with a life event that month or two consecutive months without emotional contact, at 60\% coverage. Differences in outcomes between interventions therefore combine ``how much input'' with ``the mechanism itself'' and cannot be read directly as one mechanism being better than another.

\subsection{Data calibration}\label{sec:data}
The profile generator is anchored dimension by dimension to real surveys: age band, sex (63.8\% female), self-rated health, a five-level loneliness baseline, number of surviving children, and the share of children who visit regularly (78.7\%) come from the CLHLS 2021 living-alone subsample ($n=1707$); child proximity comes from CHARLS 2015. Extroversion and the number and strength of neighbour and friend ties have no survey anchor and are modelling assumptions. Longitudinal targets (three-year loneliness onset 27.2\% and persistence 53.3\% among those living alone) were also extracted from Harmonized CHARLS 2011--2018; they are used for the dynamics calibration and held-out check in Section~\ref{sec:calib}. Main-batch profiles were verified to be byte-identical to profiles regenerated from the survey distributions (seeds 1--3). \textbf{Known bias}: CLHLS oversamples the oldest-old, and age bands are sampled here from the unweighted distribution (69.4\% aged 80+), so the profiles skew old; reweighting with the CHARLS weighted age distribution is left for future work. Outcome-definition mappings are documented in the appendix and repository. \textbf{Scope of the calibration}: what is anchored is only the \emph{marginal distribution} of all 20 residents' profiles, not the contact process. Rules-layer elders' monthly contact is generated by bands of child proximity and regular-visit status, but the monthly contact count for each band is a modelling assumption, not calibrated to survey data; focal elders' contact is generated by LLM behaviour (Section 3.1), and their baseline isolation rate (\FocIvNone\%) far exceeds that of the rules layer (\RulIvNone\%). Strictly, therefore, only the marginal profile distribution of the 20 residents is aligned with the surveys; neither the rules-layer contact frequencies nor the LLM-layer contact dynamics are calibrated. There are two output-side external checks: the tie-survival curve does not match the Burt fit (Section~\ref{sec:honest}); and after calibration of loneliness dynamics in the rules layer, the held-out three-year persistence error is 4.6 pp (Section~\ref{sec:calib}). The latter is a post-hoc calibration; the main batch ran on the default, uncalibrated dynamics, so the numbers in Section~5 are unaffected. \textbf{Profile source of rules-layer scans.} The timing and dose scan, the harness loop, and the decay-rate and post\_persist scans in Section~\ref{sec:mechanism} originally used placeholder profiles (fixed assumed parameters for every dimension). On 2026-10-21 the timing, decay and post\_persist scans were rerun with calibrated profiles and keep the same directions (\texttt{tests/*\_calibrated.json}); the tables report the original runs, and the comparison is in Section~\ref{sec:calib}.

\subsection{Preregistration and validity gates}\label{sec:prereg}
Hypotheses (H1--H4), primary and secondary outcomes, seeds, and validity criteria were written into script headers before the formal batch was launched:
\textbf{primary outcome} isolation\_rate (monthly isolation rate, defined as the share of elders with no emotional-support contact in the month); \textbf{secondary outcome} avg\_loneliness; deep\_isolation\_rate enters sensitivity analyses only, and avg\_active\_ties is descriptive only. \textbf{Validity gates}: a run must complete 24 months, have non-empty decision records, focal-elder silence rate $\le$10\%, and LLM call error rate $\le$5\%; any violation renders the run invalid and triggers a full rerun, with invalid runs archived rather than deleted. With $n=3$ seeds we perform no significance tests and claim no stable ranking; H4 is a within-run paired contrast (post-withdrawal $-$ intervention window) and is flagged as exploratory.

\textbf{Nature and limits of the primary outcome.} isolation\_rate is a monthly snapshot of ``was there any emotional contact this month''. Any presence-based service delivered monthly (casework triggers one emotional contact with probability 0.8 for each covered elder each month) drives it to zero by definition, so within the intervention window the indicator mainly measures ``is the service present''; the step changes at months 7 and 19 in Fig.~\ref{fig:traj} arise from this. Loneliness $\ell$ is a continuous quantity with homeostatic dynamics (each month it moves halfway toward a set point determined by contact volume, tie count, health, and personality) and is closer to a genuine outcome. The preregistration lists it as secondary; we do not alter that status, but we \textbf{report the two side by side} and, when interpreting effect sizes, treat loneliness as the outcome and the isolation rate as the ``presence'' indicator.

\section{Experimental Design}
Four conditions (none / casework / timebank / platform) $\times$ 3 seeds $\times$ 24 months, targeting \TargetRuns\ valid runs, of which \ValidRuns\ were frozen: platform seed~2 was ruled invalid because the focal elders' silence rate reached 11.3\% (17/150 agent-months), above the 10\% gate. A 2026-10-20 trace review shows all 150 agent-steps completed, the LLM error rate was 0, and the 17 silent months were spread across five elders rather than a gateway-timeout hole. A same-configuration rerun on 2026-10-19 reached 60.7\% silence (91/150) with an LLM error rate of 0; the trace shows elders asking the environment whether they could contact others, the read-only router answering ``no'', and the elders then ending the step. This is a routing design problem (Section~\ref{sec:robust}), not a model trait. That rerun is also invalid and is not folded into the main results, so the platform arm is reported with $n=\NPlatform$; the intervention window spans months 7--18, with post-withdrawal observation in months 19--24. Hypotheses (quoted verbatim from the preregistration, where ``casework'' was described as case management; the name was narrowed to match the actual implementation after the formal batch, see Section~\ref{sec:interventions}):
\textbf{H1} all three interventions lower the isolation rate during the window, with casework lowering it most;
\textbf{H2} loneliness improves in the same direction during the window;
\textbf{H3} the three interventions leave distinguishable behavioural signatures (help-seeking routes, pairing, volunteer matching);
\textbf{H4} (exploratory) casework rebounds more than the timebank after withdrawal---structural mutual aid is more sustainable (preregistered wording reproduced verbatim; Section~\ref{sec:results} explains that this pattern is fixed by explicit mechanism assumptions and is not read as a finding. The ``rebound'' mixes in differences in in-window level---a weak intervention has little room to rebound---so we compare sustainability by post-withdrawal level and its residual relative to the never-treated arm; rebound is listed in tables only).

\subsection{In-window effects (H1, H2)}
During the window, casework yields an isolation rate of \IsoIvCasework\%, a relative reduction of \CaseworkIvReduction\ against the \IsoIvNone\% baseline; the same-seed paired difference is \PairIvCasework pp (paired $n=$\PairIvCaseworkN). The timebank (\IsoIvTimebank\%) and platform (\IsoIvPlatform\%) improve outcomes only modestly. Loneliness (reported alongside) moves in the same direction but with a much smaller magnitude: casework \LonIvCasework\% vs.\ baseline \LonIvNone\% (a relative reduction of about 22\%, against \CaseworkIvReduction\ for the isolation rate)---the gap between the two indicators is itself evidence that the isolation rate mainly measures service presence. This ordering follows the presence effects set by the parameters: a visit counts as emotional contact with probability 0.8 for the social worker, 0.6 for timebank recipients and 0.3 for the platform, and the platform only dispatches after an event or two months without contact; loneliness falls with emotional contact through the steady-state formula. H1/H2 are therefore fixed by parameters in this model and do not constitute independent support.

\subsection{Post-withdrawal sustainability (H4)}
After withdrawal, the casework isolation rate rebounds by \IsoRebCasework pp (same-seed paired difference \PairRebCasework pp), all but erasing the in-window advantage; the timebank rebounds by \IsoRebTimebank pp (Figs.~\ref{fig:traj} and~\ref{fig:paired}). A 20-seed rules-layer scan shows the same shape (casework rebound \RulesRebCwLo\ to \RulesRebCwHi pp, timebank \RulesRebTb pp).

\textbf{Result source.} Both mechanisms are written into the environment. Casework's emotional contact is triggered directly by the social worker's monthly visit and disappears on withdrawal; timebank pairings continue to interact after withdrawal with probability post\_persist (midpoint 0.5), and the code comment explicitly labels this persistence probability ``the key mechanism assumption for H4''. LLM focal elders and rules-layer elders inhabit the same environment and share the same indicator definition, so the rules layer and the LLM layer are \emph{not} two independent lines of evidence, and agreement between them does not constitute cross-layer corroboration. The accurate statement of H4 is: \textbf{under explicitly encoded mechanism assumptions, the simulation reproduces the expected sustainability divergence and provides its sensitivity range} (caseload 4--12 does not alter the rebound; the post\_persist scan follows). Its value lies in turning the practice intuition ``dependency-type support vanishes on exit, reciprocal ties partly persist'' into a parameterised, refutable model---not in proving it true.

\textbf{The post\_persist $=0$ control (rules layer, \PPSeeds\ seeds).} To test directly whether the divergence is manufactured by this parameter, we scanned post\_persist $\in\{0,0.3,0.5,0.7,1.0\}$ in the rules layer (Table~\ref{tab:pp}). The result has two halves. (1) \textbf{Post-withdrawal level}: at post\_persist $=0$ the timebank's post-withdrawal isolation rate is \PPPostTbZero\%, identical to casework (\PPPostCw\%)---\emph{once pairings do not persist, the timebank has no sustainability advantage whatsoever}; as post\_persist rises to 0.5/0.7 the post-withdrawal level falls to \PPPostTbMid\%/\PPPostTbHi\%, so the advantage is supplied entirely by the parameter. (2) \textbf{Rebound magnitude}: even at post\_persist $=0$ the timebank rebounds less (\PPRebTbZero pp) than casework (\PPRebCw pp; \PPLtCwZero/\PPSeeds\ seeds), but this is arithmetic: the timebank's in-window effect is weaker (\PPIvTb\% vs.\ \PPIvCw\%), leaving less room to rebound, and both return to the same level. \textbf{Comparing sustainability by rebound magnitude therefore systematically favours weak interventions}; the correct quantity is the post-withdrawal level or the residual effect relative to baseline, and our main table (Table~\ref{tab:main}) reports both. A side observation: both interventions' post-withdrawal levels (\PPPostCw\%) lie below never-intervened (\PPPostNone\%), reflecting slowly decaying residual tie strength built during the window---again a direct consequence of $\delta$.

\subsection{Layer decomposition}\label{sec:layers}

\subsection{Cross-layer discrepancy}
The in-window ranking is not fully consistent across layers: the rules layer puts timebank above platform, while the LLM layer puts platform slightly above timebank (platform has only \NPlatform\ valid seeds; the difference cannot be separated from seed noise). \section{Mechanism Scans: Timing, Dosage and the Propose--Evaluate Loop}\label{sec:mechanism}
\subsection{Entry timing}
A preregistered rules-layer scan (start month $\{3,5,7,9,12\}\times$ duration $\{6,12,18\}\times$ 3 conditions, \TimingSeeds\ seeds; baseline full-horizon isolation rate \RulesBaselineOverall\%) yields three findings:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Interventions act immediately}: within the window, the isolation rate barely varies with start month---the entire cost of delay comes from unprotected waiting months, not from ``missing a critical window'';
  \item \textbf{A directional estimate of delay cost}: each month of postponement raises the two-year mean isolation rate by about \DelayCostCasework pp (casework) / \DelayCostTimebank pp (timebank), about \DelayCumCasework\ pp (casework) over 24 months against a \RulesBaselineOverall\% baseline; the platform's total effect is weak, so it is timing-insensitive (\DelayCostPlatform pp). The magnitude is fixed entirely by the rule property ``interventions act immediately'' (Table~\ref{tab:prescribed}) and is offered only as a directional estimate under the rules-layer mechanism;
  \item \textbf{Rebound is dose-independent}: extending casework from 6 to 12 months does not soften the post-withdrawal rebound (\RulesRebCwLo\ to \RulesRebCwHi pp; the independent post\_persist scan measures \PPRebCw pp, so the two scans corroborate each other)---the rebound of dependency-type support is a property of the mechanism, not a symptom of underdosing.
\end{enumerate}
Disclosures: a later start implies a shorter post-withdrawal observation window (a confound), and the 18-month duration has no post-window at all; rules-layer absolute levels are not compared with the LLM batch---only direction and magnitude are used. An LLM-behavioural-layer timing contrast across prevention / early-warning / crisis stages has \emph{not} been run; it is future work, and we do not write plans as results.

\subsection{Propose--evaluate loop (harness)}\label{sec:harness}
We demonstrate an automated research loop under preregistration constraints: an LLM designer proposes candidate configurations round by round within a preregistered search space (condition $\times$ start month 3--12 $\times$ duration 6--12, objective: minimise full-horizon isolation rate, resource cap duration $\le$12), a deterministic rules-layer evaluator executes them, and all \LoopIters\ rounds are logged to disk. The loop converges to \LoopBestDesc\ (full-horizon isolation rate \LoopBestScore\%), far within one seed standard deviation of the best grid point under the resource cap (start month 3, 12 months: 7.72\% $\pm$ 2.29); a rerun on 2026-10-22 under the original preregistration found the same optimum, as did a rerun with calibrated profiles (8.08\%, \texttt{tests/harness\_loop\_result\_calibrated.json})---\textbf{the loop works as a procedure, and we report candidly that it rediscovered the grid's plateau without surpassing it}. The guardrails (LLM proposes only; evaluation is deterministic; space and round count preregistered; every round logged) ensure the demonstration introduces no selective reporting. In addition, the per-run automated analysis--reflection mechanism (analyze.py) currently implements ``detect and flag'': any change to the experimental configuration requires human review, preventing mid-experiment design drift. Note that the loop only searches intervention schedules under fixed environment rules: it does not modify model parameters, nor does it carry lessons from one round into the next automatically. It shows that the loop runs and that selective reporting is blocked, not that the tool improves itself. Section~\ref{sec:calib} switches the proposal target to dynamics parameters with a held-out acceptance check, the next stage of this loop.

\subsection{Profile ablation and dynamics calibration}\label{sec:calib}
To answer ``what do survey-based profiles buy over ordinary random ones?'', we ran a preregistered profile ablation in the rules layer (\texttt{tests/scan\_profile\_ablation.py}; design and criteria committed in the script header before running): three profile sources, 20 seeds each, 20 elders per seed---\emph{calibrated} (CLHLS marginals sampled dimension by dimension, as in the main batch), \emph{uniform random} (anchored dimensions drawn uniformly over the same ranges), and \emph{record resampling} (whole CLHLS respondents resampled, preserving cross-dimension correlation); non-anchored dimensions are identical across sources. Input fidelity is the five-dimension total variation distance; on the output side the no-intervention condition runs 48 months (12-month burn-in), and the primary metric is the mean absolute error over four targets: cross-sectional loneliness shares (15\%, 40\%), three-year onset (27.2\%) and persistence (53.3\%).

The results come in two steps (Table~\ref{tab:ablation}). \textbf{Before calibration}, calibrated and resampled profiles clearly beat uniform ones on input fidelity but not on output: all three sources show about 98\% three-year persistence, i.e.\ loneliness states hardly change on their own, so the error comes from dynamics rather than profiles. We therefore added a monthly individual shock parameter (default 0; past results bit-identical) and fitted it with the propose--evaluate loop over 72 preregistered candidates (\texttt{tools/calibrate\_dynamics.py}), using the first three targets for training and persistence as the held-out target, on seeds 101--120. Training error fell from 12.7 to 4.8 pp and \textbf{held-out error from 43.1 to 4.6 pp}; the Spearman correlation between training and held-out error across candidates is 0.46, so both preregistered acceptance checks pass. The selected point lies on the grid boundary, and by preregistration the grid was not extended afterwards. \textbf{After calibration}, rerunning the ablation on seeds 1--20, the two survey-anchored sources beat uniform random on output (4.3 and 4.2 vs.\ 7.3 pp). The dynamics were fitted only on calibrated profiles, which favours that source; but the resampled source, not used in fitting, reaches the same error, so the advantage is not only a fitting artefact. Across all sources, before and after calibration, the in-window ranking is casework $>$ timebank $>$ platform, with magnitudes that vary by source (about 1.5 times larger under uniform random): the direction is robust to the profile source, the magnitude depends on inputs.

\subsection{Structural limits of tie decay}\label{sec:decay}
Measuring our model's tie-survival curves against Burt's yardstick (rules layer, 50 persons $\times$ 48 months) reveals two \textbf{mechanism-level limits}: (1) tie loss is markedly slower than in the literature (4-year survival \TieSurvFourYr\% vs.\ 38.3\% under Burt's fit); (2) there is a survival floor: scanning $\delta$ from 0.06 to \TieFloorDecay, 4-year survival bottoms out at \TieFloor\% (at $\delta=\TieFloorDecay$ it is already \TieFloorYrOne\% after year 1 and flat thereafter), still above Burt's 38.3\%---children with fixed contact schedules and high-frequency neighbours refresh their contact timestamps monthly, never enter the decay branch, and thus no value of $\delta$ can reproduce the sustained decline of Burt's power function (\texttt{tests/scan\_tie\_survival\_floor.py}). Accordingly we \textbf{do not treat tie-loss rate as a calibration target}, only as a sensitivity dimension. One correction attaches to the Burt citation: the paper's ``2.63-year half-life for ties outside the family'' is an arithmetic slip (a constant term left unsubtracted); recomputed from the paper's own equation it is 1.63 years\citep{burt2000decay}. The deep-isolation measure is truncated by the survival floor and therefore enters sensitivity analyses only.

\subsection{Modelling choices handled as scans}
Three independent literature searches (the third via a multi-source aggregator covering CrossRef/OpenAlex/arXiv) concurred: no authoritative source supports $\delta=0.06$ or caseload $=8$---search results that conjured ``perfectly matching'' numbers (the project encountered one fabricated reference and one fabricated statistic in its first search round) were all rejected and archived. These two parameters are therefore treated as \textbf{modelling choices}: $\delta$ is scanned over 0.06/0.15/0.25 (scan points chosen from the measured survival curves); caseload in this model effectively controls intervention coverage of high-risk elders (4/8/12 mapping to 40\%/80\%/100\%), a different unit from real-world client rosters---measured average rosters in New York City home-bound elder case management run around 75 clients per worker and are considered overloaded\citep{pardasani2018caseload}. The Chinese industry standard specifies staffing ratios but no caseload clause\citep{mzt064}, and NASW explicitly declines to set a uniform caseload standard\citep{nasw2013}, so the scan is expressed in coverage terms. Scan conclusions: $\delta$ shifts overall levels but does not reorder the interventions; caseload is monotone within the window and converges after withdrawal---coverage affects the in-place effect, not sustainability.

\subsection{Root-cause diagnosis of failed runs}\label{sec:failure}
Every run failing the validity gates (Section~\ref{sec:prereg}) was archived and diagnosed, revealing two failure modes: (1) \textbf{infrastructure failures}---LLM gateway error storms blacking out whole months of decisions (errors visibly clustered in the trace), handled by rerunning with a higher retry count (an infrastructure parameter, not a protocol change); (2) \textbf{interface failures}---LLM calls succeed with zero errors, but after a read-only query returns a false ``you cannot contact anyone'', the elder stops acting. We earlier classified this as ``behavioural silence'' and read it as a model trait; a trace review (\texttt{ROUTER\_INCIDENT\_20261021.md}) confirms it is a routing design problem (Section~\ref{sec:robust}). The remedy is fixing the router; runs before the fix are judged by the original gate, are not folded into the main results, and the gate is unchanged.

\subsection{Model and inference-setting disclosure}
The formal batch uses a single model (gpt-5.6-sol) with uniform sampling settings; reasoning-effort level was not recorded by the run script and is disclosed as ``not recorded'', to be supplemented with a small-sample contrast. Cross-model robustness (a gpt-6-astra contrast batch: the none and casework runs at seed~1) is treated as a sensitivity check outside the main results; see Section~\ref{sec:robust}.

\subsection{Cross-model comparison and the routing incident}\label{sec:robust}
We swap the focal elders' driving model for gpt-6-astra, keeping every other setting (profiles, seed, intervention parameters, horizon) identical to the seed-1 main-batch runs; the plan was to compare the casework$-$none direction. That comparison is compromised by the routing incident below, so \textbf{no valid cross-model contrast is currently available}.

\textbf{Routing incident.} Elder models cannot call environment tools directly; they can only ask the environment a natural-language question. When a question is marked read-only, the code-generation router hands only the read-only tools (observing one's own situation, the community report) to another model, which writes an answer that is passed back verbatim. The write tools (calling, visiting, asking for help) are registered, but the prompt never asked the generator to distinguish ``cannot write in this call'' from ``this function does not exist'', so answers often said ``you cannot call or visit anyone''. Real elders face no such step. gpt-5.6-sol mostly issues orders without asking first and so stays under 10\% silence on the same router; gpt-6-astra asks more often and treats the answer as a real constraint, yet writes decisions when a social worker visits monthly and the task becomes concrete. This matches OpenAI's system card for the model: Astra is stronger at respecting safety and authorization boundaries, with unauthorized transactions in 6.8\% of realistic work-environment cases without a confirmation policy versus 38.0\% for GPT-5.6 Sol\citep{openai2026astracard}. The same card reports lower chain-of-thought monitorability than Sol; we did not measure this, and only note that for more boundary-respecting models, judging ``unwillingness to act'' from action logs alone may be insufficient without checking what the environment told them. Here the cross-model comparison did what a single model could not: Sol issued orders directly and masked the router defect, while Astra asked first, stopped, and exposed it. The router was fixed on 2026-10-20: questions about whether a function exists are answered from the registry (``the tool exists; this read-only call cannot invoke it; use readonly=false to act''), and any generated answer still claiming the function is missing is replaced and not cached.

\textbf{Effect on existing results.} Eight gpt-6-astra runs used the old router: none seed~1 twice in September 2026 (silence 10.7\% and \McAstraSilNone\%, both invalid), casework seed~1 once (\McAstraSilCw\%, valid), and five runs on 19 October (silence about 31--87\%, never a complete four-condition set). Under the 10\% gate only the September casework seed~1 is valid. The same ``cannot contact'' answer appears in the gpt-5.6-sol platform seed~2 rerun (60.7\% silence), so it is not a tendency unique to one model. We withdraw the earlier reading of astra's silence as a model trait (``less action without visitors, decisions pulled out by visits''). The astra rows of Table~\ref{tab:robust} are an incident record, not a cross-model comparison. New-router gpt-6-astra runs for the four seed-1 conditions are under way; they will be compared only with old-router astra seed~1 (before vs.\ after the fix), not mixed with the old-router Sol batch.

\textbf{Note on validity gates.} A silence gate mixes faults, interface problems and model differences; before attributing ``doing nothing'' to a model, check whether environment replies told the agent a false capability boundary.

\section{Cost Accounting}\label{sec:cost}
Cost reporting for multi-agent LLM experiments must include runs eliminated by quality gates. This project accumulated \TotalCalls\ LLM calls (measured from traces, archived invalid runs included), with an estimated total cost of \$\CostTotal: \$\CostValid\ on valid main-batch runs, \$\CostWasted\ sunk on invalid ones (a \WasteShare\ share of the main batch), plus \$\CostModelCompare\ for the cross-model contrast arms (Section~\ref{sec:robust}). Token usage is a documented assumption (traces do not record per-call usage) and unit prices follow public pricing; the full accounting basis is released with the data files. Here the layered design pays off concretely: the rules-layer scans (\TimingSeeds\ seeds $\times$ 45 configurations) and the harness loop cost zero API dollars; running the same scans through the LLM layer would exceed the main batch's cost by an order of magnitude.

\section{Core Conclusions and Innovations}\label{sec:core}
\textbf{Core conclusions.} (1) Generating profiles from survey distributions is necessary but not sufficient: with anchored profiles alone, outputs are no closer to reality than with ordinary random profiles, and the error comes mainly from loneliness dynamics; once dynamics are calibrated under preregistration and accepted on held-out targets, survey-anchored profiles beat random ones on output (Section~\ref{sec:calib}). (2) Under this model's parameters, the in-window ranking is social-worker visits $>$ mutual-aid pairing $>$ volunteer matching; the direction is robust to the profile source while the magnitude depends on inputs; social-worker visits fall back to the never-treated level after withdrawal, and the post-withdrawal advantage of mutual-aid pairing is set entirely by the pairing-persistence parameter (Section~\ref{sec:results}). (3) Under rules-layer mechanics, entry timing shows up as a delay cost accumulating linearly by month rather than as a ``missed critical window'' (Section~\ref{sec:mechanism}).

\textbf{A methodological finding.} The cross-model comparison exposed an environment defect that a single model could not: Sol issued orders directly and masked the router's habit of phrasing ``cannot write in this call'' as ``cannot contact anyone'', while the more boundary-respecting Astra asked first, stopped, and exposed it (Section~\ref{sec:robust}). ``Doing nothing'' in LLM social simulation should be attributed to the model or the simulated person only after checking what the environment told the agent.

\textbf{Innovations.} (1) Turning ``is the simulation merely an echo of its rules?'' into a testable question: a result-source ledger item by item, and zeroing and scanning experiments that take key conclusions apart; (2) pushing survey anchoring from ``do the profiles look right?'' to ``are the outputs right?'': a preregistered ablation plus train/held-out calibration, reporting the negative pre-calibration result as well; (3) a layered architecture that makes large scans free of API cost while keeping the behaviour and reasons of an LLM focal layer; (4) organising the comparison around the social-work, social-security and social-governance channels so that simulation questions map onto practice decisions.

\section{Discussion: Implications for Practice}\label{sec:discussion}
\textbf{(1) Social-worker visits need a built-in exit plan.} The strongest in-window effect and the post-withdrawal fall-back are two faces of one mechanism: the social worker is an injected active node, contact ends at exit, and the fall-back does not soften with longer service. Exit design---gradual tapering and referral into reciprocal networks such as mutual-aid pairing---should be a standard component of outreach.

\textbf{(2) The sustainability of mutual-aid pairing rests on an unmeasured quantity.} In the model its post-withdrawal advantage comes entirely from the pairing-persistence parameter and vanishes when it is zeroed. ``Social workers open the way, mutual-aid pairs carry on'' is therefore only a hypothesis; whether it holds depends on how often real pairings persist after a programme ends, which the time-bank literature has not measured\citep{lu2024timebank}. Measuring it would settle the policy value of such programmes more decisively than another outcome evaluation.

\textbf{(3) Waiting time is a design variable.} Under rules-layer mechanics, delay cost accumulates monthly (about \DelayCostCasework pp per month for social-worker visits). This magnitude rests on the assumption that an intervention produces contact as soon as it starts; real start-up lags and coverage ramps would change it.

\textbf{(4) Secure intensity before tuning timing.} Volunteer matching has a weak total effect and is insensitive to timing; optimising timing for weak interventions adds little.

All of the above are model projections for generating testable practice hypotheses; \textbf{we claim no causal effects in real populations}.

\section{Conclusion}
We propose and demonstrate a layered agent-based simulation method for research on social-work interventions: an LLM focal layer embedded in a rules-layer network, survey-anchored profiles validated on held-out targets, and externalised mechanism parameters, together with preregistration, validity gates, root-cause classification, cost accounting and a result-source ledger. The case of older adults living alone shows how the sandbox is used: the presence effects of social-worker visits, mutual-aid pairing and volunteer matching, the fall-back after withdrawal, the dependence of pairing persistence on a single parameter, and the delay cost of entry timing can all be projected under explicit parameters and tested by zeroing or scanning. Every number updates automatically from the scripts; we invite reproduction per the appendix.

\section{Limitations and Ethics}\label{sec:limits}
\textbf{Limitations.} (1) $n=3$ seeds ($n=\NPlatform$ for the platform arm) support only descriptive contrasts; (2) profiles match only survey marginals; loneliness dynamics were calibrated post hoc in the rules layer only (selected point on the grid boundary), contact frequencies and tie decay remain modelling choices, the tie-survival curve fails the Burt check, and the LLM main batch ran on pre-calibration dynamics; (3) intervention intensities and inputs are modelling choices not aligned with literature effect sizes or service volumes, so the ranking among interventions is set by parameters; (4) the primary outcome is a monthly contact snapshot and loneliness follows a steady-state formula, so neither is an independent outcome; (5) macro results come mainly from six LLM elders and are sensitive to model behaviour and silence, and the withdrawal window is only six months; (6) the rules-layer timing scan and the harness loop search only under fixed rules and do not modify model parameters; (7) the main batch ran on the pre-fix router: focal elders who queried the environment could receive a false ``cannot contact'' answer, so although valid Sol runs stay at 4.0--10.0\% silence, some silent or low-action months may stem from it, and focal-layer results should be read accordingly. Follow-up work is listed in the parameter appendix and \texttt{DATA\_MACHINE\_RUNBOOK.md} in the repository.

\textbf{Ethics.} All agents are simulated entities; no real individuals are involved. Survey data enter only as aggregate distributions from public microdata. The LLM-generated ``elders' rationales'' are mechanistic probes, not real elders' voices, and the paper avoids quoting them anthropomorphically. Because simulation conclusions could be misused as direct grounds for resource allocation, we repeatedly stress their hypothesis-generating character.

\section{Validity-Gate Adjudication Rules}
A run is valid if and only if: the DONE flag exists; monthly metrics for months 0--24 are complete; decision records are non-empty; the six focal elders' combined silence rate is $\le$10\% (strictly greater than 0.10 fails); and the LLM call error rate is $\le$5\%. Invalid runs are archived under timestamped directories (\texttt{*.invalid-<timestamp>}) with all traces and decision logs preserved for diagnosis---never deleted.
